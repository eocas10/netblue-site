It's Monday morning. Your receptionist turns on her computer and can't open a single file. A message on the screen says the company's data has been encrypted, and the only way to get it back is to pay. That's ransomware, and it isn't just a big-company problem. Small offices are attractive targets precisely because they tend to have fewer defenses and depend completely on their systems to bill, see patients or close sales.

The good news is that most attacks come in through the same few doors, and closing them doesn't take a corporate budget. This guide explains how an attack works, which measures stop it, and what to do if it happens to you.

How ransomware gets into an office

Attackers rarely need sophisticated hacking. They take advantage of everyday gaps:

  • Phishing emails with an attachment or link that installs malware or steals an employee's password.
  • Stolen or reused passwords for email, VPN or remote desktop, with no two-step verification.
  • Unpatched equipment: firewalls, servers and computers with known vulnerabilities that already have a fix.
  • Remote access exposed to the internet, such as Windows Remote Desktop (RDP) left open.

Once inside, attackers often move through the network for days, look for backups to delete and, when everything is ready, encrypt the files. Many groups also copy the data first and threaten to publish it.

Five measures that cut the risk the most

1. Backups the attacker can't reach

This is what decides whether an attack is a disaster or just a bad day. Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of your data, on two different media, with one off-site. At least one copy should be offline or immutable, meaning it can't be deleted or changed from the office network. Most important of all: test that you can restore. A backup you've never restored is an assumption, not a plan.

2. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) everywhere

With MFA, a stolen password isn't enough to get in. Turn it on first for email, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, your VPN and any remote access tool.

3. Keep everything updated

Many attacks use vulnerabilities the vendor fixed months ago. Keep Windows, the firewall, the router, the server and your applications current. A simple, regular patching routine keeps one forgotten device from becoming the way in.

4. Employees who can spot a scam

Phishing targets people, not machines. Short, practical training with real examples of fake emails greatly reduces risky clicks. Just as important is a culture where reporting a suspicious email is easy and nobody is afraid to do it.

5. Fewer privileges and a segmented network

If every employee logs in as an administrator, ransomware gets administrator rights too. Use standard accounts for daily work and separate your network: guest Wi-Fi, payment devices and servers shouldn't share the same segment. That way, if one computer is infected, the damage stays contained.

What to do if you're hit

  1. Disconnect affected devices from the network (cable and Wi-Fi), but don't turn them off: they may hold useful evidence.
  2. Don't delete anything or try to “clean it up” yourself.
  3. Call your IT provider to contain the attack and assess the damage.
  4. Report it. In the U.S. and Puerto Rico you can report it to the FBI at ic3.gov.
  5. Check your legal obligations. If you handle patient, customer or card data, the law may require you to notify those affected within a set time.
  6. Restore from clean backups once you've confirmed the attacker no longer has access.

Should you pay the ransom?

Authorities advise against it. Paying doesn't guarantee you'll get your data back or that it won't be leaked, and it marks you as a target that pays. In some cases, paying certain groups can also create legal problems. The best way to avoid facing that decision is to have backups that work.

Where to start this week

You don't have to do everything at once. If you can only take three actions, make them these: confirm you have an off-site backup and test restoring a file, turn on MFA for everyone's email, and make sure remote desktop isn't exposed to the internet.
